### Phase screens method {#sec:ps}
Analytical approaches to wave propagation in random media rely on many restrictive assumptions^[@charnotskii2015], which limit their applicability to weak and sometimes moderate turbulence.
Moreover, ensemble averaging inherent to these analytical treatments provides only statistical quantities of the optical field, such as mean intensity, correlation functions or scintillation index, leaving us without access to individual realizations of beam spots and their spatial dynamics.

The phase screen method^[@martin1988] is one of the standard numerical techniques for modeling wave propagation through a randomly inhomogeneous medium such as atmospheric turbulence.
The main idea is to represent the effect of turbulence along the propagation path as a sequence of thin phase-modulating layers separated by free-space propagation intervals.
Each screen imposes a random phase distortion corresponding to the integrated refractive index fluctuations over a short segment of the path, while free-space propagation between screens accounts for diffraction and interference effects.

#### Split step operator.
To formalize this idea we rewrite the paraxial wave equation ^[eq:parax] in the form of the first-order differential equation with an operator on the right-hand side
$$\frac{\partial u(\mathbf{r};z)}{\partial z}=\bigg[\,\underbrace{\frac{i}{2k}\Delta_\mathbf{r}}_{\hat H_d} +\underbrace{ik\,\delta n(\mathbf{r},z)\vphantom{\frac{1}{1}}}_{\hat{H}_t(z)}\,\bigg] u(\mathbf{r};z) \equiv \hat H(z)\, u(\mathbf{r};z)$$
where $H_d$ corresponds for contribution from diffraction, $H_t(z)$ corresponds for contribution from turbulence $H(z)=H_d + H_t(z)$. In general, as the derivative and the function, they don't commute: $H_d H_t\neq H_t H_d$.
The solution to this type of equations can be formally written in the operator form
$$u(\mathbf{r};z) = \tau\,\mathrm{exp}\left[\int_{0}^{z}\hat H(z')dz'\right] u(\mathbf{r}; 0)$$
where  $\tau$ denotes the space-ordering operator, introduced by analogy with the Dyson series, as
$$
\begin{split}
\tau \exp\left[\int_{0}^{z} \hat{H}(z')dz'\right] = 1 + \int_{0}^{z} \hat{H}(z') dz' + \int_0^z \hat{H}(z') dz'\int_0^{z'} \hat{H}(z'') dz'' + \\
+ \int_0^z \hat{H}(z') dz'\int_0^{z'} \hat{H}(z'') dz''\int_0^{z''} \hat{H}(z''') dz''' + \dots
\end{split}
$$
where $z > z' > z'' > z''' > \dots$.
We expand it to the second order in $z$
$$
\begin{split}
\tau \exp\left[\int_{0}^{z} {H}(z')dz'\right] =
\textcolor{green}{1 + {H}_d z + \int_0^z {H}_t(z') dz'} + \\ +
\textcolor{green}{\frac{1}{2} H_d^2 z^2}+
\textcolor{red}{H_d\int_0^zdz'\int_0^{z'}H_t(z'')dz''} + \\ +
\textcolor{red}{\int_0^z H_t(z') H_d z' dz'} + \textcolor{green}{\int_0^z H_t(z') dz' \int_0^{z'} H_t(z'') dz''} + O(z^3)
\end{split}
$$
Let's consider the following ansatz (with regular exponentials) and expand it to the second order
$$
\begin{split}
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}H_dz\right) \exp\!\left(\int_0^z H_t(z')dz'\right) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}H_dz\right) = \\ =
\textcolor{green}{1 + H_dz + \int_0^z H_t(z')dz' + \frac{1}{2}H_d^2z^2} + \textcolor{red}{\frac{1}{2}\bigg[H_d\,z \int_0^z H_t(z')dz' +} \\
+\textcolor{red}{z\int_0^z H_t(z') H_d dz'} + \textcolor{green}{2\int_0^z H_t(z') dz' \int_0^{z'} H_t(z'') dz''} \bigg] + O(z^3)
\end{split}
$$
comparing it with the previous expression we can write
$$
\begin{split}
\tau \exp\left[\int_{0}^{z} \hat{H}(z')dz'\right] &= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}H_dz\right) \exp\!\left(\int_0^z H_t(z')dz'\right) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}H_dz\right) + \\ &+
\textcolor{red}{H_d\int_0^zdz'\int_0^{z'}H_t(z'')dz'' + \int_0^z H_t(z') H_d z' dz' -} \\ &\textcolor{red}{- \frac{1}{2}H_d\,z \int_0^z H_t(z')dz' - z\int_0^z H_t(z') H_d dz'} + O(z^3)
\end{split}
$$
Then, if the turbulence term can be represented as a Taylor series in $z$ as $H_t(z) = H_t(0) + H'_t(0) z + H''_t(0)z^2 + \dots$  then all the integral terms are vanished out (for the $0$-th order of $H_t$ series) or become of order $O(z^3)$.
Finally we can write
$$
\boxed{\begin{split}
u(\mathbf{r};z) \approx \underbrace{\exp\!\left(\frac{z}{2}\frac{i}{2k}\Delta_\mathbf{r}\right)}_{\text{vacuum prop. on } z/2} \exp\!\!\left(i\underbrace{k\!\int_0^z \delta n(\mathbf{r},z')dz'}_{\phi(\mathbf{r}) \text{ - phase screen}}\right) \underbrace{\exp\!\left(\frac{z}{2}\frac{i}{2k}\Delta_\mathbf{r}\right)}_{\text{vacuum prop. on } z/2} u(\mathbf{r}; 0)
\end{split}}
$$
That means that for light beam propagation in turbulent atmosphere we can decouple the diffraction impact and the turbulence impact with the 2-d order accuracy by $z$.
As the result, the light field amplitude of the beam at the distance $z$ can be obtained by propagating the initial field $u(\mathbf{r};0)$ like in vacuum on the half of the total distance, applying the integrated phase perturbation induced by index of refraction perturbation and propagating this perturbed field again in vacuum on distance $z/2$.

The accumulated phase delay caused by refractive index fluctuations within a segment of propagation distance $z$ is referred to as the phase screen $\phi(\mathbf{r})$
$$
%\label{eq:phasescreen}
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf{r})=k\!\int_0^z \delta n(\mathbf{r},z')dz'}$$
It is simple to see that $\left<\phi(\mathbf{r})\right>=0$ and $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ is a stationary random field in the 2D space of $\mathbf{r}=(x,y)$.
To define the power spectral function $\Phi_\phi$ let's consider the correlation function
$$B_\phi(\boldsymbol r = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1) = k^2 \int_0^z\!\! dz_1 \!\int_0^z\!\! dz_2 \left<\delta n(\mathbf{r_1}, z_1) \delta n(\mathbf{r_2}, z_2) \right>$$
Considering the case when the phase-screen thickness $z$ is much greater than the longitudinal correlation length of the refractive-index fluctuations, we can assume that correlations along the propagation direction are averaged out. This allows us to apply the Markov approximation^[@martin1988]
$$\begin{split}
\left<\delta n(\mathbf{r_1}, z_1) \delta n(\mathbf{r_2}, z_2) \right> \simeq& \,\delta(z_1 - z_2)\, \int_{-\infty}^\infty dz' B_n(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1, z')
\end{split}$$
Rewriting both correlation functions in terms of spectral density functions and integrating the delta function we obtain
$$\iint d^2\boldsymbol\kappa \,\Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf r} = k^2 \int_0^z dz_1 \, 2\pi \iint d^2\boldsymbol{\kappa} \Phi_n(\boldsymbol\kappa, \kappa_z=0)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf r}$$
which means that the spectral density function of the phase screen is defined as the following
$$\boxed{\Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol\kappa) = 2\pi\, k^2z\, \Phi_n(\boldsymbol\kappa, \kappa_z\!=0)}$$

Since the refractive-index fluctuations can be assumed as a Gaussian random field ^[@tatarskii1971], and the phase screen $\phi$ is a linear map of this field ^[eq:phasescreen], the phase screen itself is Gaussian.
In this case, the power spectral density function $\Phi_\phi$ fully characterizes the phase screen.

#### Basic method of phase screens generation.
For the numerical generation of phase screens, it is common to employ the Fourier–Stieltjes decomposition^[eq:FSint].
This simplifies the problem because instead of direct sampling spatially correlated random variables we need to sample independent random variables correspond to spectral modes.
In this section, we describe the widely used method for phase-screen generation^[@frehlich2000] utilized in classical optics in turbulent atmosphere and discuss its limitation.

The approach originates from the necessity to employ the Fast Fourier Transform^[@cooley1965] -- a highly optimized algorithm for computing Fourier transforms -- in order to accelerate the numerical transformation of spectral mode samples into spatial variables.
This imposes a constraint that the spatial coordinates and the spectral modes must be defined on a rectangular grid of size $N\times N$ as:
$$\mathbf{r}_{nm}=\left(n\Delta x, m\Delta x \right)\quad n = -\frac{N}{2}, \dots, \frac{N}{2}-1, \quad m = -\frac{N}{2}, \dots, \frac{N}{2}-1$$
where $L$ is the spatial size of the phase screen, $\Delta x = L / N$ is the spatial grid sampling spacing, and
$$\boldsymbol\kappa_{pq} = \left(p\Delta\kappa, q\Delta\kappa \right)\quad p = -\frac{N}{2}, \dots, \frac{N}{2}-1, \quad q = -\frac{N}{2}, \dots, \frac{N}{2}-1$$
where $\Delta\kappa = \frac{2\pi}{L}$  is the spectral grid sampling spacing.

The phase screen is then approximated as
$$\phi(\mathbf{r}_{nm}) \approx \sum_{p=-N/2}^{N/2-1}\sum_{q=-N/2}^{N/2-1} a_{pq} e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa_{pq}\mathbf{r}_{nm}}$$
where the spectral coefficients $a_{pq}$ are sampled as independent zero-mean complex Gaussian random variables $a_{pq} \sim \mathcal{CN}\!\left(0,\, \Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol{\kappa_{pq}} )(\Delta\kappa)^2\right)$ and must satisfy the Hermitian symmetry property $a^*_{pq}=a_{-p-q}$ in order to produce the real valued phase screen.

The parameters of the grid $L$ and $N$ must be chosen carefully as they determine the minimal and maximal spatial frequencies that can be represented.
According to the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem^[@shannon1949], the highest spatial frequency that can be resolved is $\kappa_\text{Nyquist} = N \Delta\kappa / 2$.
This imposes the limitation that the spatial sampling step $\Delta x = L / N \lesssim l_0 / 2$, where $l_0$ is the inner scale of the turbulence (see ^[sec:turb_cascade]).
On the other hand, the lowest spectral component of interest is of the order of $2\pi / L_0$, where $L_0$ is the outer scale of the turbulence.
This leads to the requirement $\Delta\kappa \lesssim 2\pi/L_0$ which is practically impossible to satisfy with current computational resources.
For example, for the typical values $l_0 \sim 3\text{mm}$ and $L_0 \sim 80\text{m}$ the required grid parameters are $L=80\text{m}$ and $N\sim 5\times 10^4$.
Consequently, in practical simulations the grid size $L$ is usually chosen to be several times larger than the beam diameter in order to avoid boundary effects and ensure that the wavefront distortions are fully contained within the computational window.
The grid resolution $N$ is then selected as a compromise between accurately representing small-scale fluctuations and maintaining feasible computational cost.
The accuracy of the chosen parameters can be verified by comparing the simulated phase structure function $D_{\phi}(\rho)$^[eq:struct_func] with its theoretical form.
And it reveals that this method often exhibit a known undersampling effect at lower frequencies (larger spatial distances).
We will provide a detailed analysis and verification of this phenomenon in^[sec:verification].
Thus, this method requires certain corrections to be suitable for practical use.

#### Subharmonic method.

There are bunch of approaches that try to improve sampling at low frequencies domain.
One approach to compensate for missing low-frequencies components in generated phase screens is the subharmonic method, as described by Lane et al.^[@lane1992].
This method introduces additional low-frequency components by explicitly adding random Fourier modes in the undersampled spectral region $[-\Delta\kappa, \Delta\kappa]^2$.
Typically, the corresponding low-frequency phase screen is very small, e.g., $3×3$ points.
The resulting low-frequency phase screen is added to the main phase screen.

>An example of such subdivision is showed in the figure^[fig:subharmonics].
>![[Pasted image 20251025144108.png]]

To further increase the effective screen size, the same procedure can be applied recursively to the low-frequency screen in the region $[-\Delta\kappa/3, \Delta\kappa/3]^2$ introducing additional large-scale components corresponding to a larger spatial period.
Repeating this procedure $d$ times yields a phase screen with an effective size of $3^d L$, progressively capturing increasingly larger turbulence scales.

>Figure^[fig:psstrfunc] illustrates the improvement of the phase structure function achieved by this method, compared to the basic approach, for different orders of low-frequency phase screens.

While the subharmonic correction method drastically improve the representation of low-frequency statistics over the basic method, the resulting structure function isn't close enough to the theoretical one for practical orders of subharmonics (see^[sec:verification]).
Moreover, using a fixed rectangular grid for spectral modes introduces biased statistics, which does not match the continuous nature of atmospheric turbulence spectra.
These factors, combined with the significant improvements in computational performance over recent decades, have motivated the development of new methods that move beyond the basic method of phase screen generation.

#### Sparse spectrum method.
The Sparse spectrum method, introduced by Charnotskii in 2013^[@charnotskii2013], moves out from Fast Fourier Transform method for phase screen generation thus allows arbitrary choosing of spectral components.
More over, for each realization of phase screen these spectral components are randomly chosen.
With proper sampling of spectral modes and their amplitudes, this method perfectly reproduces the required structural function of the phase screen.

Similar to the basic method, a phase screen $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ is represented as sum of finite number independent harmonic oscillators
$$
%\label{eq:sparsespectrum_ps}
\phi(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{Re}\sum_{n=1}^N a_n e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa_n\mathbf{r}}$$
where the spectral coefficients $a_{n}$ are sampled as zero-mean independent complex Gaussian random variables with $\left<|a_n|^2\right> = s_n$, and $\boldsymbol\kappa_n$ are random variables with probability distribution $p_n(\boldsymbol\kappa)$.
The real part is taken because of the relaxation of the Hermitian symmetry restriction on $a_n$ coefficients.

Comparing the structure function of the phase screen generated by^[eq:sparsespectrum_ps] with the targeted
$$\begin{split}
D_\phi(\boldsymbol{\rho})&=\left<\left(\phi(\mathbf{r}+\boldsymbol\rho) - \phi(\mathbf{r})\right)^2\right>_{\left\{a_n, \boldsymbol\kappa_n\right\}} = \\ &= \iint d^2\boldsymbol\kappa \sum_{n=1}^N s_n p_n(\boldsymbol\kappa) (1 - \cos(\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf r)) \equiv 2\iint d^2\boldsymbol\kappa \Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)(1 - \cos(\boldsymbol\kappa\mathbf r))
\end{split}$$
leads to the following constraint
$$
%\label{eq:snpn}
\sum_{n=1}^N s_n p_n(\boldsymbol\kappa) = 2\Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)$$
This is the only constraint required to obtain required spectral density, leaving us with arbitrary choosing of $\boldsymbol\kappa_n$ distributions.
For example, this generalization reduces to the basic phase screen method when $p_n(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ are the delta functions at the nodes of the rectangular grid.
Considering the rotational symmetry of $\Phi_\phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)$ and its logarithmic scale, it looks natural to sample the modes uniformly in logarithmic, rotationally symmetric coordinates.
In addition, for such a log-uniform partitioning, it was shown that a higher than structure function, the two-point fourth moment, corresponds to the desired Gaussian statistics^[@charnotskii2013a].

To simplify computation, let us split the spectral space on $N$ non-overlapping rings and assign the distribution domain of each $\boldsymbol\kappa_n, n=1\dots N$ to a single ring $K_{n-1} < \kappa < K_n$, where
$$K_n = K_\text{min}\exp\!\left[\frac{n}{N}\ln\left(\frac{K_\text{max}}{K_\text{min}}\right)\right]$$
$K_\text{min}$ and $K_\text{max}$ are the spectral range bounds. Typical values are $K_\text{min}=\kappa_0$ and $K_\text{max}=2\kappa_m$ (see ^[sec:turb]).
With such non-overlapping partition, we integrate the eq^[eq:snpn] for each ring and obtain that
$$s_n = 4\pi\int_{K_{n-1}}^{K_n} d\kappa\,\kappa\, \Phi_\phi(\kappa)$$
with the $p_n(\boldsymbol\kappa) = 2\Phi_\phi(\kappa)s^{-1}_n$. To simplify computation, we can approximate the distributions of $\boldsymbol\kappa_n$ with uniform distributions on the corresponding rings. This means that for each realization of the phase screen, we sample $\boldsymbol\kappa_n$ as follows:
$$|\boldsymbol\kappa_n| = \sqrt{K_{n-1}^2+\xi_n(K_{n}^2-K_{n-1}^2)}$$
where $\xi_n\in[0,1]$ are uniform i.i.d random variables, and polar angles are uniform i.i.d random variables on $[0,2\pi]$.

As the result, the sparse spectrum method provides an approach to generate phase screens with unbiased structure function in general and with minimal discrepancy with the approximation of uniform sampling on rings, as will be verified in ^[sec:verification].
Despite the direct summation of modes instead of Fast Fourier transform, the computational performance is comparable with the basic method because of smaller number of modes.
Because of random sampling of modes, is even possible to achieve unbiased structure function with $N=1$.
But for better high order statistics^[@charnotskii2013a] the typical value is $N\sim500$, compared to the value of $\sim500^2$ for the basic method.

Another key advantage of our approach is the independence between the spatial grid and the choice of spectral modes.
In the basic method, one parameter determines the other, which restricts the size of generated phase screens to only a few meters.
In contrast, the sparse spectrum method enables the generation of arbitrarily long phase screens while preserving the correct statistical properties.
This, in turn, makes it possible to investigate time-dependent effects in the propagation of quantum light through a turbulent atmosphere.
